## MOCK EXAM QUESTION 1
### Where are the historical roots of the European integration process?
*(Circumstances / Founding countries / Initial aims)*

**CIRCUMSTANCES — WHY did integration happen?**
- WWII devastation: two world wars in 30 years killed tens of millions; European states could no longer guarantee their citizens' security alone
- Key insight: France and Germany had gone to war three times in 70 years (1870, 1914, 1939). The root cause was economic rivalry — especially over the Ruhr/Saar industrial regions (coal + steel)
- US Marshall Plan (1947) required European cooperation as a condition for aid — external pressure toward integration
- Cold War context: Soviet threat required Western solidarity; US strongly encouraged European unity as a buffer
- End of empire: European states were losing colonies; needed new sources of economic dynamism
- Green (2010): Democratic peace theory — liberal democracies bound by trade do not go to war with each other. Integration = institutionalised peace.

**FOUNDING COUNTRIES — The Six:**
- France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, Netherlands, Luxembourg
- First step: European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) 1951 — placed coal and steel production (war materials) under joint supranational authority (Schuman Plan)
- Signed: Treaty of Paris 1951
- Rationale: if Germany and France share control of steel/coal, neither can secretly rearm for war

**INITIAL AIMS:**
- Treaty of Rome 1957 → created EEC (European Economic Community) and Euratom
- EEC aimed from the start not just at a free trade area but at a common market — free movement of goods, services, capital, labour
- The original six explicitly intended economic integration to lead toward political union in the long run
- Contrast with EFTA (European Free Trade Association) — set up 1960 by the "outer seven" (UK, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Austria, Switzerland, Portugal) who only wanted a free trade area, not supranational ambitions
- The EEC vs. EFTA split was the first major divergence: deeper integration (EEC) vs. lighter cooperation (EFTA)
- Peace was the primary motivation; economic prosperity was the mechanism

**KEY DATES:**
- 1951: ECSC Treaty of Paris (coal + steel, 6 founding members)
- 1957: Treaty of Rome → EEC + Euratom
- 1973: First enlargement (UK, Denmark, Ireland join)
- 1986: Single European Act → committed to completing the Single Market by 1992
- 1992: Maastricht Treaty → created the EU; set up EMU roadmap; renamed EEC to EC
- 1999: Euro launched (accounting); 2002: Euro banknotes/coins
- 2004: Big Bang enlargement (10 new members, mostly Eastern Europe)

## MOCK EXAM QUESTION 2
### EU Single Market — main freedoms and obstacles to their full potential

**THE FOUR FREEDOMS (Common Market = Balassa Stage 3):**

| Freedom | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Free movement of goods** | No tariffs, quotas, or equivalent restrictions on goods crossing internal EU borders | German cars sold in Poland without import duty |
| **Free movement of services** | Companies can offer services across borders; professionals can practice anywhere | Czech architect working on a project in Austria |
| **Free movement of capital** | No restrictions on investment across borders; no capital controls | French bank buying a Spanish savings bank |
| **Free movement of labour** | EU citizens can live and work in any member state | Polish plumber working in the UK (before Brexit) |

**LEGAL BASIS:**
- Single European Act (1986) committed to completing the single market by 1 January 1993
- Requires harmonisation of product standards, mutual recognition of qualifications, removal of non-tariff barriers

**OBSTACLES TO FULL POTENTIAL:**

*Goods (most complete freedom):*
- Technical standards diverge (e.g., electrical plugs, safety requirements) despite harmonisation efforts
- Country-of-origin labelling, packaging requirements
- Public procurement often still favours national suppliers

*Services (most incomplete freedom — this is the big gap):*
- Services Directive (Bolkestein Directive, 2006) was controversial and significantly watered down
- Professional qualifications not always mutually recognised — a lawyer qualified in France cannot automatically practice in Germany
- National licensing regimes for regulated professions (doctors, architects, engineers)
- Banking and financial services: regulatory fragmentation; Banking Union still incomplete
- Digital services: VAT rules differ; platform regulation varies

*Capital:*
- Capital Markets Union (CMU) still in progress — EU lacks a pan-European capital market comparable to the US
- Companies seeking large-scale equity funding often go to NYSE/NASDAQ instead of EU exchanges
- EU is losing high-growth companies to US capital markets

*Labour:*
- Language barriers
- Non-portability of pension rights across countries
- Mutual recognition of qualifications still patchy
- Political resistance (see UK before Brexit — "Polish plumber" controversy)

**DYNAMIC GAINS FROM THE SINGLE MARKET (Sapir):**
1. *Economies of scale*: firms can reach minimum efficient scale impossible in small protected national markets
2. *Competition*: EU rivals force prices down, reduce X-inefficiency
3. *FDI/investment*: large stable market attracts capital inflows

**KEY STAT TO USE:** EU Single Market = ~450 million consumers; world's largest integrated market by GDP

## MOCK EXAM QUESTION 3
### EU Institutions — the critical institutional trio for EU decision-making

**THE THREE KEY INSTITUTIONS:**

### 1. European Commission (the executive / proposer)
- **Who:** 27 Commissioners (one per country) + President (Ursula von der Leyen, 2024–2029, second mandate — max two mandates)
- **Sole right to propose legislation** — nothing can become EU law without a Commission proposal
- Guardian of the treaties — can take member states to court for non-compliance
- Manages EU budget execution
- Commissioners do NOT represent national interests — they represent the EU as a whole
- Communicates via **green papers** (consultation) → **white papers** (policy intent) → formal legislative proposal
- 27 Directorates-General (DGs) covering all policy areas

### 2. Council of the EU (the member states)
- **Who:** Ministers from each member state (composition changes by topic — energy ministers for energy, finance ministers for finance, etc.)
- Also called "the Council of Ministers"
- **Co-legislates** with the European Parliament on all Commission proposals
- **Voting:** Most legislation requires **Qualified Majority Voting (QMV)**:
  - Majority of member states (at least 15 of 27) **AND**
  - At least **55% of states** representing at least **65% of total EU population**
  - Some sensitive areas (tax, foreign policy, treaties) require **unanimity**
- **Presidency:** Rotates every 6 months by country (Czech Republic held it during both 2009 gas crisis and recent energy crisis)

### 3. European Parliament (directly elected / co-legislator)
- **Who:** 720+ MEPs directly elected every 5 years by EU citizens
- Seats: max 96 per country (Germany 96; Czech Republic 21)
- MEPs sit in **European fractions** (cross-national political groupings), not national delegations
- Largest fraction post-2024: European People's Party (~24%)
- **Co-legislates** with Council of EU; approves EU budget; votes to approve Commission President
- Only institution where citizens have a **direct vote**
- Problem: Low turnout — Czech EP turnout ~18–28%; Eastern Europe generally low

### HOW THEY WORK TOGETHER — THE TRIALOGUE:
```
Commission PROPOSES
        ↓
Council of EU + European Parliament NEGOTIATE (trialogue)
        ↓
Law AGREED (ordinary legislative procedure)
```
- The trialogue is the three-way informal negotiation between Commission, Council of EU, and Parliament
- Most EU secondary legislation is produced this way

### TYPES OF EU LAW:
| Type | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Regulation** | Directly applicable in all member states exactly as written; **no national transposition needed** |
| **Directive** | Sets a goal; member states choose HOW to achieve it; **must be transposed into national law** |
| **Decision** | Binding only on specific addressees (a country, a company, a person) |

### THE FOURTH INSTITUTION — EUROPEAN COUNCIL (don't confuse!):
- **Who:** Heads of government of all member states + its own President (Antonio Costa, current)
- **Role:** Sets strategic priorities; does NOT co-legislate
- Makes decisions by consensus; handles foreign policy, budget frameworks, crisis responses
- The EUCO simulation in class was based on this institution

### KEY DISTINCTIONS (exam trap):
| European Council | Council of the EU |
|---|---|
| Heads of government | Ministers |
| Strategic direction | Legislative body |
| Does not pass laws | Co-legislates with Parliament |
| Meets as summits | Meets by policy area |

## MOCK EXAM QUESTION 4
### EU Budget — NextGenerationEU vs. MFF; what's special about NGEU for Czechia

**THE MULTIANNUAL FINANCIAL FRAMEWORK (MFF):**
- The EU's long-term budget — sets spending ceilings for 7-year periods
- Current MFF: 2021–2027 (approx. €1.2 trillion)
- Financed by: customs duties, share of national VAT, share of GNI (the biggest source), plastic packaging levy (new)
- The EU cannot run deficits in its regular budget — it must be balanced

**NEXTGENERATIONEU (NGEU):**
- Launched in response to COVID-19 pandemic (2020)
- Total size: **€750 billion** (2018 prices); ~€807 billion in current prices
- Split: ~€390 billion in **grants** (don't need to be repaid), ~€360 billion in **loans**
- Main instrument: **Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF)** — €672.5 billion
- Countries submit National Recovery and Resilience Plans (NRRPs) and receive funds tied to **milestones and targets**

**WHAT IS SPECIAL ABOUT NGEU?**
1. **Unprecedented borrowing:** For the first time, the EU Commission borrowed money on capital markets on behalf of the EU as a whole — issuing EU bonds (not national bonds). This was a historic step toward fiscal integration.
2. **Joint liability:** All member states stand behind the debt. Weaker economies benefit from the EU's AAA credit rating → lower interest rates than they could get alone.
3. **Grants, not just loans:** The grant component does not need to be repaid by recipient countries.

**WHAT IS SPECIAL FOR CZECHIA specifically:**
- Czechia is a net recipient — it receives more from the EU budget than it contributes
- NGEU gives Czechia access to low-cost financing at EU-level rates (better than Czech government bonds)
- The grant component is essentially a transfer — Czechia gets money it doesn't repay directly
- Czechia's NRRP focuses on: green transition, digitalisation, resilience
- Political controversy: Czechia (under Babiš governments) had issues with EU funds due to conflict of interest concerns (Agrofert conglomerate); some funds were frozen
- The Babiš government had complicated relations with NGEU conditionality (rule of law, anti-corruption milestones)

**RELATION BETWEEN MFF AND NGEU:**
- They run in parallel but are separate instruments
- MFF = regular long-term budget (structural funds, CAP, cohesion, etc.)
- NGEU = exceptional one-off instrument on top of the MFF, financed by EU-level borrowing
- NGEU will be repaid 2028–2058 via future EU budgets (new own resources being negotiated)
- Together they form the **"EU Recovery Package"** — sometimes called "Bazooka" response to COVID

## MOCK EXAM QUESTION 5
### EU Trade Policy — framework; "Open Strategic Autonomy"

**THE FRAMEWORK OF EU TRADE POLICY:**
- Trade policy is an **exclusive EU competence** — member states have NO individual trade policy; the Commission negotiates trade agreements on behalf of all 27
- This means: the EU speaks with one voice in the WTO, in free trade negotiations, and on tariffs
- The EU is the world's largest trading bloc by trade volume
- The Common External Tariff (CET): all goods entering the EU face the same tariff regardless of which member state they enter through
- Trade agreements negotiated by the Commission, ratified by the European Parliament and Council

**KEY EU TRADE AGREEMENTS:**
- CETA (Canada, 2017 — provisionally applied)
- EPA with Japan (2019)
- Mercosur agreement (negotiated but not yet ratified)
- Ongoing: India, Australia, etc.

**OPEN STRATEGIC AUTONOMY (OSA):**
- The EU's response to the twin shocks of COVID and the war in Ukraine
- The concept: the EU should be **open to trade and investment** (not protectionist) BUT must **reduce strategic dependencies** on single sources for critical goods
- "Open" = maintain free trade orientation, reject protectionism
- "Strategic" = identify sectors where dependency is dangerous (semiconductors, rare earths, pharmaceuticals, energy)
- "Autonomy" = build capacity to act independently even if partners are unreliable

**WHERE OSA SHOWS UP IN POLICY:**
- **Chips Act** (EU CHIPS Act, 2023): €43 billion to boost EU semiconductor production; goal to double EU chip market share by 2030
- **Critical Raw Materials Act** (2024): reduce dependence on China for lithium, cobalt, etc.
- **Net-Zero Industry Act**: build EU manufacturing capacity in clean tech
- **Foreign Subsidies Regulation**: prevent non-EU companies subsidised by their governments from distorting the EU market
- **Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM)**: prevent carbon leakage (see Q7)

**THE TENSION:**
- OSA creates tension with WTO rules and with partners (US, China complain EU is becoming protectionist)
- The Draghi Report (2024) also highlighted EU's competitiveness deficit and called for more investment, not protectionism
- Key phrase to use: *"The EU seeks to remain a champion of open trade while building resilience against strategic vulnerabilities."*

## MOCK EXAM QUESTION 6
### EU Energy Policy — shared competence; relation to decarbonization and climate

**WHY ENERGY IS A SHARED COMPETENCE:**
- Energy policy appears in the Treaty of Lisbon (2007) under **Article 194 TFEU**
- It is a **shared competence**: both the EU and member states can legislate, but where the EU has legislated, national law must align
- Why shared (not exclusive)?
  - Energy **mix** (how much nuclear, coal, gas, renewables a country uses) is a **national sovereign choice** — explicitly protected in Article 194
  - Historical reason: energy = security; states do not want to fully surrender energy decisions to Brussels
  - Different countries have very different starting points (France = nuclear, Poland = coal, Denmark = wind, Norway = hydro)

**WHAT THE EU CAN DO (shared part):**
- Set **renewable energy targets** (Renewable Energy Directive — RED III: 42.5% renewable by 2030)
- Set **energy efficiency standards** (Energy Efficiency Directive)
- Regulate internal energy markets (3rd Energy Package, Clean Energy Package)
- Build interconnectors and energy infrastructure (TEN-E regulation)
- Respond to emergencies (gas solidarity mechanism, joint gas procurement)

**WHAT MEMBER STATES RETAIN:**
- The right to choose their own energy mix
- Nuclear policy (France keeps reactors; Germany closed all; neither is forced)
- Ownership structure of energy companies

**RELATION TO DECARBONIZATION AND CLIMATE:**
- EU climate targets (European Green Deal) require phasing out fossil fuels → energy system must decarbonize
- **Fit for 55** package (2021): set of laws to cut emissions by 55% by 2030 vs. 1990
- This creates **tension** between the energy sovereignty of member states and EU-wide climate goals:
  - Poland still ~70% coal-dependent → EU pushing for coal phase-out but cannot force the pace
  - Germany's Energiewende (energy transition): closed nuclear, built renewables, but became more gas-dependent on Russia → disaster when Russia invaded Ukraine
- **REPowerEU** (2022): after Ukraine invasion, accelerate renewable energy deployment + reduce Russian gas dependency (Russia supplied ~40% of EU gas before 2022)

**KEY INSTRUMENTS linking energy and climate:**
- EU ETS covers energy sector (power plants) — price signal to shift from fossil fuels
- Renewable energy mandates push deployment of wind/solar
- Energy poverty concerns: rapid decarbonization raises energy costs → social implications (addressed in Social Climate Fund)

## MOCK EXAM QUESTION 7
### EU Climate Policy — EU ETS and CBAM; relation between the two

**EU ETS (Emissions Trading System):**
- Launched **2005** — first and still largest carbon market in the world
- How it works: **cap-and-trade**
  - EU sets a total **cap** on emissions from covered sectors
  - Companies receive or buy **allowances** (EUAs — EU Allowances); 1 EUA = right to emit 1 tonne of CO2
  - Companies that emit less can **sell** surplus allowances; companies that emit more must **buy** more
  - The cap decreases each year → total emissions fall; carbon price rises as scarcity increases
- **Covered sectors:** power generation, heavy industry (steel, cement, aluminium), aviation (intra-EU), and from 2024: shipping
- **ETS 2:** Separate new ETS for road transport and buildings (fuel suppliers), launching 2027
- From EEI lecture: Green paper 2000 → launched 2005; EU was global front-runner on carbon pricing
- Post-COVID: carbon price rose sharply (€80–100/tonne range); provides strong investment signal

**CARBON BORDER ADJUSTMENT MECHANISM (CBAM):**
- Adopted 2023; transitional phase 2023–2025; full implementation from **2026**
- The problem it solves: **carbon leakage**
  - If EU companies pay €80/tonne for carbon, but competitors in countries with no carbon price pay nothing, then EU producers are at a disadvantage
  - Companies might move production to non-EU countries = carbon leakage (emissions don't disappear, they just move outside the EU's jurisdiction)
- How CBAM works:
  - Importers of certain goods from non-EU countries must buy **CBAM certificates** corresponding to the carbon price they WOULD have paid if produced under the EU ETS
  - If the exporter already paid a carbon price in their home country, that is deducted
  - Starting with: **cement, steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity, hydrogen**
- CBAM essentially **extends the EU ETS price to imports** from countries without equivalent carbon pricing

**THE RELATION BETWEEN ETS AND CBAM:**
- CBAM is the **border mirror** of the ETS:
  - ETS: EU producers pay for carbon
  - CBAM: non-EU exporters to the EU also pay for carbon (at the border)
  - Together: they level the playing field and prevent carbon leakage
- As free ETS allowances for covered industries are phased out (2026–2034), CBAM phases in fully
- They are explicitly designed to be **complementary** — phasing out free allowances and phasing in CBAM happen in parallel
- CBAM is also an element of **Open Strategic Autonomy** — it protects EU industry's competitiveness while maintaining ambition on climate
- WTO compatibility debate: some countries argue CBAM is a trade barrier; EU argues it's a carbon pricing equivalence measure

**FORMULA FOR EXAM:**
> *"The EU ETS caps and prices carbon emissions within the EU. CBAM extends that carbon price to imports, preventing carbon leakage and protecting EU producers' competitiveness. Together they form an integrated climate-trade instrument."*

## MOCK EXAM QUESTION 8
### EU Economic Union — the Stability and Growth Pact; fiscal or monetary?

**THE STABILITY AND GROWTH PACT (SGP):**
- Agreed 1997 (in force 1998), came into operation with the euro 1999
- Purpose: ensure fiscal discipline among eurozone member states — prevent any country from running excessive deficits that could destabilise the euro

**THE RULES (the famous numbers):**
- **Government deficit** must not exceed **3% of GDP**
- **Government debt** must not exceed **60% of GDP** (or be declining toward it at a satisfactory pace)
- These come from the **Maastricht convergence criteria** (also needed to join the euro)

**THE SGP IS ABOUT FISCAL POLICY (not monetary):**
- Monetary policy in the eurozone is controlled by the ECB — it is already unified. No SGP needed for monetary because it's already centralised.
- The problem the SGP addresses is **fiscal**: member states still run their own national budgets (tax and spend independently)
- Risk: if a country runs large deficits, it may create debt that threatens the stability of the whole eurozone → contagion risk (see Q9 — sovereign debt crisis)
- The SGP was the compromise solution to the "fiscal union vs. no fiscal union" debate: rather than creating a common EU budget authority, member states agreed to rules on their own spending

**HOW ENFORCEMENT WORKS:**
- European Commission monitors national budgets
- **Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP):** if a country breaches the 3% rule, the Commission recommends corrective action; the Council can impose fines
- In practice: fines were never imposed (political difficulty); rules were suspended for COVID (2020–2024)

**REFORMED SGP (2024):**
- The old SGP was widely seen as too rigid and one-size-fits-all
- New rules (adopted 2024): more country-specific debt adjustment paths; greater flexibility; focus on debt sustainability rather than mechanical deficit ceiling
- The 3%/60% benchmarks remain but with more nuanced enforcement

**MONETARY POLICY — THE ECB:**
- **Primary mandate:** price stability — inflation target ~2%
- ECB controls interest rates for all 19 (now 20) eurozone countries
- **Monetary trilemma:** Cannot simultaneously have (1) fixed exchange rate, (2) free capital movement, and (3) independent monetary policy
  - Eurozone countries: chose (1) + (2), gave up (3)
  - Czechia: retained (3) — keeps its own central bank and koruna

**THE CORE TENSION:**
- Eurozone = common monetary policy (ECB) + independent fiscal policies (member states, constrained by SGP)
- This is NOT an optimal currency area: asymmetric shocks hit countries differently but they can't use monetary policy to respond
- Balassa and others argued: monetary union without fiscal union is unstable → proved correct in 2010–2012 (Q9)

## MOCK EXAM QUESTION 9
### The European Sovereign Debt Crisis — build-up and crisis

**THE BUILD-UP (pre-2008):**
- Euro creation (1999) compressed interest rate spreads — Greek, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese bonds were priced almost identically to German bonds
- Low borrowing costs led peripheral countries to borrow heavily:
  - Greece: borrowed for public spending; grew government deficit
  - Spain/Ireland: borrowed for private-sector construction boom (not government)
  - Portugal: slow growth; persistent current account deficits
- Market participants assumed (incorrectly) there was an implicit EU bailout guarantee → mispriced risk
- The "no bailout" clause in EU treaties (Art. 125 TFEU) was assumed to be unenforceable

**THE TRIGGER (2007–2009):**
- US subprime crisis (2007–2008) → global financial crisis → Lehman Brothers collapse (September 2008)
- Global recession hit eurozone hard; automatic stabilisers kicked in (spending up, tax revenues down) → deficits jumped everywhere
- Greek government (2009): revised deficit figures upward dramatically — real deficit was ~15% of GDP, not the 6% previously reported
- Markets panicked: bond spreads on Greek debt spiked; cost of borrowing rose sharply

**THE CRISIS (2010–2012):**
- **Greece (2010):** First bailout — €110 billion from IMF + eurozone member states; conditions = brutal austerity
  - Problem: austerity deepened the recession → tax revenues fell → deficit didn't close as fast as projected → second bailout needed (2012: €130 billion)
- **Ireland (2010):** Banking crisis (not fiscal) — government guaranteed bank liabilities → sovereign debt crisis; €85 billion bailout
- **Portugal (2011):** €78 billion bailout
- **Spain (2012):** €100 billion bank recapitalisation aid (partial bailout for banking sector)
- **Cyprus (2012–2013):** Bailout including "haircut" on bank deposits above €100,000 (unprecedented)

**THE INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE:**
- **European Financial Stability Facility (EFSF)** — temporary bailout mechanism, 2010
- **European Stability Mechanism (ESM)** — permanent bailout mechanism, 2012 (replaced EFSF)
- **ECB:** Mario Draghi's "whatever it takes" speech (July 2012) — promised to buy government bonds to protect the euro. Markets calmed immediately. Never actually had to buy bonds. Most important monetary policy statement in modern EU history.
- **Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT):** ECB programme announced to buy sovereign bonds of distressed countries if they sign an ESM adjustment programme
- **Fiscal Compact (2012):** Treaty requiring member states to enshrine balanced budget rules in national law
- **Banking Union:** began construction — SSM (Single Supervisory Mechanism) in ECB, Single Resolution Mechanism

**THE DEEPER CAUSES (structural):**
- Eurozone = monetary union without fiscal union → no automatic stabiliser mechanism (no "federal budget" to redistribute from booming to busting regions)
- No lender of last resort for sovereigns (ECB's mandate didn't explicitly allow it) → self-fulfilling crisis: if markets expect default, borrowing cost rises → makes default more likely
- Competitiveness divergence: Germany ran surpluses; periphery ran deficits; common exchange rate prevented adjustment
- Moral hazard: markets assumed bailout would happen; encouraged excessive borrowing pre-crisis

**WHAT BALASSA PREDICTED:**
- Balassa (1961, cited by Sapir 2011) warned that monetary union without fiscal union would be unstable — countries losing the exchange rate tool need something else (fiscal transfers or labour mobility) to adjust to asymmetric shocks. The debt crisis confirmed this.

## MOCK EXAM QUESTION 10
### Levels of the Economic Integration Process — with EU examples

**BALASSA'S FIVE STAGES (1961):**

| Stage | Definition | EU Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Free Trade Area** | Member countries remove tariffs and quotas between themselves; each keeps own external tariffs | EFTA (1960): UK, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Austria, Switzerland, Portugal — only wanted this |
| **2. Customs Union** | FTA + common external tariff toward non-members | EEC from 1968: Common External Tariff introduced; EU customs union today |
| **3. Common Market** | Customs Union + free movement of goods, services, capital, AND labour | EU Single Market (1993): four freedoms; established by Single European Act (1986) |
| **4. Economic Union** | Common Market + harmonised economic policies (fiscal, monetary, structural) | EU today: common competition policy, SGP, EMU for eurozone members |
| **5. Political Union** | Economic Union + common foreign/defence policy + supranational government | Not yet achieved by EU; the end-point aspiration |

**THE EU'S CURRENT STATUS:**
- The EU sits at **Stage 4 — Economic Union in progress** — not fully achieved even at Stage 4
- Reasons it hasn't completed Stage 4:
  - Fiscal policy remains national (SGP constrains but does not unify)
  - Not all 27 are in the eurozone (Czechia, Sweden, Poland, Hungary, etc. still outside)
  - Banking union is incomplete
  - Capital markets union is in progress
- **It is definitely NOT a Political Union** (Stage 5): no common military (NATO does this), no common tax authority, national identities primary

**THE ORIGINAL AMBITION:**
- The EEC founders (1957) aimed at a common market from the start — they intended to go further than just a free trade area (unlike EFTA)
- The drive toward monetary union (EMU) came from desire to eliminate exchange rate risk in the common market (a company trading across 27 countries shouldn't face 27 different currencies)

**HOW TO EXPLAIN WITH EU EXAMPLES:**
- Stage 1 → Stage 2: 1968 — customs union completed; Common External Tariff in place
- Stage 2 → Stage 3: 1993 — Single Market completed (four freedoms in law, if not fully in practice)
- Stage 3 → Stage 4: 1999 — euro launched for accounting; 2002 banknotes/coins; ECB takes over monetary policy for eurozone
- Remaining step: Capital Markets Union, fiscal union (if ever) = completing Stage 4 and moving toward Stage 5

**STATIC vs. DYNAMIC EFFECTS (Sapir — directly cited in lecture as exam material):**

*Static effects (one-time welfare effects):*
- **Trade creation (+):** Domestic production replaced by cheaper imports from EU partners; consumer welfare gain
- **Trade diversion (−):** Common external tariff diverts trade from cheap non-member (e.g., Asia) to pricier EU partner; welfare loss
- Net welfare effect = trade creation minus trade diversion → EU historically positive overall

*Dynamic effects (ongoing, compound over time):*
1. **Economies of scale:** Firms can expand beyond small protected national markets; reach minimum efficient scale; lower average costs
2. **Competition:** Exposure to EU competitors forces prices down, eliminates X-inefficiency (slack in monopolistic firms), drives innovation
3. **Investment/FDI:** Large stable market attracts foreign direct investment; capital inflows raise productivity

*Location effects:*
- **Static (Heckscher-Ohlin):** Labour-abundant Eastern Europe attracts labour-intensive production; capital stays in the West
- **Dynamic (Krugman — New Economic Geography):** Agglomeration: firms cluster in cores (Paris, Frankfurt, Amsterdam) → self-reinforcing concentration; periphery loses unless corrected
- EU response: **Structural Funds** and **Cohesion Policy** — transfers to lagging regions to counteract agglomeration

*Short run vs. long run:*
- Short run: adjustment costs dominate — workers displaced, industries relocate, trade diversion losses
- Long run: dynamic effects compound — estimated 5–10% GDP gain attributed to 40+ years of integration

## ADDITIONAL KEY MATERIAL

### Integration Theories (likely in Q1 or comparative questions)

| Theory | Key author | Core claim | Explains | Fails on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Federalism** | Hamilton, Spinelli | States integrate because they can no longer guarantee security alone | Why integration started post-WWII | Doesn't explain incremental steps or reversals |
| **Neo-functionalism** | Haas (1958) | Cooperation in one sector spills over to others; self-sustaining | EEC → Single Market spillover | Empty Chair Crisis (1965); Brexit |
| **Intergovernmentalism** | Hoffmann (1966) | Member states are key actors; integration = interstate bargaining | Why states retain veto power | Day-to-day Commission/Parliament activity |
| **Liberal Intergovernmentalism** | Moravcsik (1998) | National preference formation → bargaining → institutional choice | Single European Act; Maastricht | Incremental governance |
| **Multi-level governance** | Marks & Hooghe (2001) | Authority flows up (to EU) AND down (to regions); horizontal too | Structural funds, regional policy | Hard to predict outcomes |

**Empty Chair Crisis (1965–66):** France under de Gaulle boycotted Council meetings for 7 months to block qualified majority voting; resolved by Luxembourg Compromise — effectively any state could veto anything by claiming "vital national interest." Showed integration is not automatic.

### EU Peace Project (Green 2010)
- Internal peace achieved: no war between EU members since 1957
- Mechanism: democratic peace theory + economic interdependence
- External limitation: EU failed to stop Yugoslav wars (1990s); relied on NATO
- EU lacks independent military capacity; defence integration still weak
- Enlargement conditionality: requiring democracy + rule of law from candidates extends peace zone eastward

### Hungary/Orbán Context (from class — current EU affairs)
- Hungary under Orbán: blocked €90B Ukraine aid package; closest Putin ally in EU; EU froze ~€18B in structural funds over rule of law
- Péter Magyar / TISZA: opposition; pro-EU; pledges to unlock frozen funds; 2030 eurozone target
- Russia alignment gradient: Hungary → Slovakia (Fico) → Czechia (pragmatic) → rest of EU
- Czech situation: Babiš coalition with SPD + Motorists; foreign agent law targeting NGOs; Charles University controversy; President Pavel (NATO veteran) sidelined by government on NATO summit

### Capital Markets Union (recurring discussion in class)
- EU losing high-growth companies to US (NYSE/NASDAQ)
- Goal: pan-EU capital market allowing larger-scale investment
- Without it: startups and scale-ups list in the US; EU misses out on the gains
- Draghi Report (2024) emphasised this as a competitiveness priority

## QUICK-REFERENCE EXAM BULLETS

**Founding year facts:**
- 1951: ECSC (6 countries, Paris Treaty)
- 1957: Treaty of Rome → EEC + Euratom
- 1968: Customs union completed
- 1986: Single European Act
- 1992: Maastricht Treaty → EU name; EMU roadmap
- 1993: Single Market completed
- 1999: Euro launched (accounting)
- 2002: Euro banknotes
- 2004: Big Bang enlargement (10 new members)
- 2007: Lisbon Treaty (reform Treaty)
- 2010–12: Sovereign debt crisis; ESM; "Whatever it takes"

**Numbers to remember:**
- SGP: 3% deficit / 60% debt (Maastricht criteria)
- NGEU: €750 billion (grants + loans)
- MFF 2021–27: ~€1.2 trillion
- QMV: 55% of states + 65% of population
- MEPs: 720+; max 96 per country (Germany); Czech Rep = 21
- ECB inflation target: ~2%
- Renewable target: 42.5% by 2030 (RED III)
- CBAM full implementation: 2026
- EU ETS launched: 2005

**Institutions — don't mix up:**
- European Council = heads of government (strategic, no laws) — President: Antonio Costa
- Council of the EU = ministers (legislative, QMV) — Presidency rotates every 6 months
- European Commission = proposes laws, guardian of treaties — President: von der Leyen
- European Parliament = directly elected, co-legislates — 720+ MEPs

**Regulation vs. Directive:**
- Regulation = directly binding, identical in all states, NO transposition needed
- Directive = goal-setting, states choose how, MUST be transposed into national law

**ETS vs. CBAM in one sentence:**
- ETS prices carbon at the production source inside the EU; CBAM prices it at the border for imports — together they prevent carbon leakage and level the playing field.

**SGP in one sentence:**
- The Stability and Growth Pact is a FISCAL (not monetary) instrument that limits member state budget deficits to 3% of GDP and debt to 60% to protect the stability of the euro.

**"Whatever it takes" = Mario Draghi, ECB, July 2012 = the turning point of the sovereign debt crisis.**

**Balassa stages in 10 seconds:**
FTA → Customs Union → Common Market → Economic Union → Political Union
(EU is currently between 4 and 5; not yet 5)
